Chapter 2 --- Astrophysics Background Knowledge¶


Before we get started on analysing the data, we thought it would be helpful to give some important astrophysics background knowledge for the topics that we are going to be exploring and analysing.


TESS:

The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) is a space telescope that was launched by NASA on April 18, 2018. It is designed to search for exoplanets, particularly focusing on discovering planets around bright, nearby stars. TESS is the successor to the Kepler mission, expanding from Kepler’s focus on one patch of the sky to surveying almost the entire sky, divided into 26 sectors.

In order to identify these exoplanets, TESS uses the transit method which involves detecting dips in a star’s brightness caused by the planets passing in front of them.

TESS has made some pretty major discoveries such as several small, rocky exoplanets in habitable zones and planets that orbit around binary star systems!

MORE INFO: https://exoplanets.nasa.gov/tess/

Source (Image Left): TESS https://space.skyrocket.de/doc\_sdat/explorer\_tess.htm

Source (Image Right): Depiction of the transit method https://www.esa.int/Science\_Exploration/Space\_Science/How\_to\_find\_an\_extrasolar\_planet


CM Draconis:

CM Draconis is an eclipsing binary star system consisting of two near-identical M-dwarf stars (very small stars!) with an orbital period of ~1.27 days. The near-identical nature of the stars, their tight orbit, and their precisely measured radii and masses, makes CM Draconis an incredibly helpful and important system in studies of stellar evolution, stellar structure, and exoplanets.

Source: CM Draconis https://www.stellarcatalog.com/stars/cm\-draconis (Check out this website, it's super cool!)


Starspots:

Starspots are temporary, darker, cooler regions on a star’s surface (photosphere) caused by intense magnetic activity. Their darker appearance indicates that they are cooler than their surrounding regions because the light emitted by an object is directly related to its temperature. This can be explained by blackbody radiation; hotter objects emit more light and at shorter, bluer wavelengths whilst cooler objects emit less light and at longer, redder wavelengths. This results in the cooler sunspots appearing darker relative to the hotter, brighter surrounding photosphere.

Source: https://aasnova.org/2018/12/12/what\-are\-starspots\-like\-on\-sun\-like\-stars/


Stellar Flares:

Stellar flares are intense bursts of electromagnetic radiation, from x-rays to radio waves, occurring on a star’s surface due to magnetic activity. They occur on most stars with outer convective zones due to the convection stirring and amplifying the magnetic fields, which eventually get twisted and explosively release energy.

Stellar flares release huge amounts of energy. This means that solar flares, stellar flares occurring on the Sun, can have significant effects on Earth. They can impact communication and navigation systems by disrupting the Earth’s ionosphere which is crucial for radio wave transmission. High-energy particles released from the flares can also interact with Earth’s magnetic field and atmosphere, creating beautiful auroras, with stronger flares pushing these auroras towards the Earth’s equator.

Source: https://public.nrao.edu/news/record\-breaking\-stellar\-flare\-from\-nearby\-star\-recorded\-in\-multiple\-wavelengths\-for\-the\-first\-time/

# We'll store the data for the flux at the time of the flares here
fluxAtFlare = []
fluxAtFlareRetro = [] #This list will store the data that Prof. Martin had access to, the original 163 flares, so we can compare our results with his

# Iterate over each entry on the flare table and estimate the corresponding flux value based on the data
for x in range(1, len(flare_table)): # We start at 1 to avoid treating the title line as data
    closestTimeIndex = 0
    flareAfterFlux = False
    for y in range(len(spot_time)):
        # Find index that produces a spot time nearest the flare time 
        if abs((spot_time[closestTimeIndex] - flare_time[x])) > abs((spot_time[y] - flare_time[x])):
            closestTimeIndex = y
            # Check to see if the flare happened before or after the time we identify. This will be useful for interpolation 
            if spot_time[closestTimeIndex] < flare_time[x]:
                flareAfterFlux = True
            else:
                flareAfterFlux = False
    
    # Now we work out the closest times on either side of the flare.
    if flareAfterFlux == True: # If the flare happened after our closest time, we need the next time
        closestTimeBefore = spot_time[closestTimeIndex]
        closestFluxBefore = spot_flux[closestTimeIndex]
        
        closestTimeAfter = spot_time[closestTimeIndex + 1]
        closestFluxAfter = spot_flux[closestTimeIndex + 1]
        
    else: # If the flare happened before our closest time, we need the time before it
        closestTimeBefore = spot_time[closestTimeIndex - 1]
        closestFluxBefore = spot_flux[closestTimeIndex -1]
            
        closestTimeAfter = spot_time[closestTimeIndex]
        closestFluxAfter = spot_flux[closestTimeIndex]

    # Now we calculate the slope between the two points, find where the flare lies, and estimate a corresponding flux value
    interpolationSlope = (closestFluxAfter - closestFluxBefore)/(closestTimeAfter - closestTimeBefore)
    
    estimatedFlux = (flare_time[x] - closestTimeBefore)*interpolationSlope + closestFluxBefore
        
        
    fluxAtFlare.append(estimatedFlux)
    
    # Keep the first 163 flares (x = 1 to 163; row 0 is the header). These are the flares from Martin et al
    if x < 164:
        fluxAtFlareRetro.append(estimatedFlux)

Chapter 4 --- Bootstrapping¶

OVERVIEW:

Bootstrapping is a statistical technique that involves resampling a single given dataset with replacement in order to create many simulated bootstrapped samples of the same size as the original dataset. For bootstrapping to be successful it is vital that we resample with replacement in order to ensure that the simulated sample mean can differ with each resample. We can then use our produced distribution of simulated samples to perform statistical analysis such as calculating standard errors and performing hypothesis tests. Bootstrapping provides us with a more accurate representation of the entire population than our smaller initial dataset, therefore improving the accuracy and reliability of our conclusions.


DEFINITIONS:

--> Population: The complete group about which some information is desired

--> Sample: An unbiased subset of the population

--> Null Hypothesis: A statistical hypothesis that claims that no statistical significance exists in a given set of observations

--> P-value: The probability that the null hypothesis produces the observed result or weirder


OUR BOOTSTRAPPING:

We can analyze how correlated flare occurrence and positive spot modulation flux are by using the bootstrapping method to derive a p-value!

In this case, we define our null hypothesis to be the assumption that there is no positive correlation between the flare occurrence and positive spot modulation flux, meaning that a flare is equally likely to occur at any given time, independent of the flux. Then, we use bootstrapping, where we randomly select 244 flares from all the fluxes that we have in our dataset, with replacement, to create a simulated sample of flare occurrences. By repeating this process multiple times, 10000 times to be exact, we create a distribution of simulated samples. For each simulated sample we then checked if we had the same number, or a greater number, of flares at positive fluxes as our observed sample, which was 148/244.

We then take the number of simulated samples for which this occurs and divide it by the total number of simulated samples to obtain our p-value! Remember, a small p-value indicates that the probability of this outcome occurring given that the null hypothesis is true is very small.

Note: This is a one-tailed test because in this case we only care about the samples that have 148 or more flares at positive fluxes.

N = 10000
n_flares = len(fluxAtFlare)        # 244 flares in total
observed_positive = positiveFluxCount  # 148 of them have positive flux (counted earlier)

num = 0
# Collect 10,000 samples of randomly selected flares
for ii in range(N):
    # Randomly draw 244 flares (with replacement!) from all fluxes in our sample
    bootstrap = np.random.choice(spot_flux, n_flares, replace = True)
    # Count how many of the randomly selected flares have a positive flux
    flares_pos_flux = np.sum(bootstrap > 0)

    # If this many or more flares correspond to a positive flux, then we count it since it is as weird as (or weirder than) what we observed
    if flares_pos_flux >= observed_positive:
        num += 1

# The chance that we get equal to or more than the observed number of flares at positive fluxes in our simulated samples is the p-value
print('Our calculated p-value is ' + str(num/N))

Bootstrapping Conclusions:

Let's sum up our findings from bootstrapping:

Null Hypothesis: There is no positive correlation between flare occurrence and positive spot modulation flux.

P-value: ~0.009 (the exact value changes a little each run because the resampling is random) --> This p-value tells us that there is a less than 1% chance of our observed result occurring, given that the null hypothesis is true. This is incredibly small and so we can conclude that there does exist a positive correlation between flare occurrence and positive spot modulation flux.

Chapter 5 --- T-test¶

OVERVIEW:

An alternate method for testing this correlation is by using a t-test. A t-test is a statistical test that compares the means of two groups. There exists both one-sample t-tests and two-sample t-tests, and which one is implemented depends on the nature of the data and the comparison being made. One-sample t-tests are used when comparing the mean of a single sample to a known population mean or theoretical expectation. This helps determine whether the sample differs significantly from the expected value. Two-sample t-tests, on the other hand, are used when comparing the means of two independent groups to assess whether they are significantly different from each other.


EQUATION: $t = \frac{\overline{x} - \mu }{ \frac{ s }{ \sqrt{n} } }$

--> $\overline{x}$ = sample mean

--> $\mu$ = assumed/ hypothesised mean

--> $s$ = standard deviation of sample

--> n = sample size


OUR T-TEST:

We can analyze how correlated flare occurrence and positive spot flux are by using a t-test to derive a p-value! To do this, we conducted a one-sample t-test, comparing the distribution of the spot modulation flux of the 244 flares to a normal distribution with a mean of zero.

The first step is to calculate the t-value using the equation above. The t-value is the ratio of the difference between the mean of the sample and the hypothesised mean, and the variation that exists within the sample sets.

Then, as we did for our bootstrapping, we define our null hypothesis to be the assumption that there is no positive correlation between the flare occurrence and positive spot modulation flux, meaning that a flare is equally likely to occur at any given time, independent of the flux. Next, we use our calculated t-value to find our p-value. In a t-test this p-value represents the probability of observing a t-value as, or more, extreme than the t-value calculated from the sample data, under the assumption that the null hypothesis is true. As was the case in our bootstrap test, a small p-value indicates that the probability of this calculated t-value occurring given that the null hypothesis is true is very small.

# Student T-test 
# Compare distribution of spot fluxes distribution with mean of zero 

# A function to run a one-sample student t-test on a distribution. Assumes that the null hypothesis mean will be 0 
def studentTTest(distribution):
    n = len(distribution)
    s = np.std(distribution,ddof=1)
    standardError = s/np.sqrt(n)
    t = np.mean(distribution)/standardError
    return t

Confidence in T-value:¶

Now that we have obtained a t-value from our t-test, let's see how confident we are in it. We can tell how confident we are by calculating the p-value of our t-value.

--> To find a p value, we must first plot something called a T-Distribution

--> The T-Distribution shows the spread of possible t-values we would calculate if we were to take new samples

--> the distribution is modeled by the following function: $f(x) = \frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)}\left(1+\frac{x^2}{\nu}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}}$

--> $\nu$ = degrees of freedom (for a one-sample t-test, $\nu = n - 1$)

--> $\Gamma(T)$ = the gamma function

T-Test Conclusions:

Let's sum up our findings from the t-test:

Null Hypothesis: There is no correlation between flare occurrence and positive spot modulation flux.

P-value: 0.206 --> This p-value tells us that there is about a 20.6% chance of seeing a t-value at least this large (one-sided, since we only care whether the mean is above zero), if we assume that the null hypothesis is true. This is not sufficient to reject the null hypothesis, and thus is not sufficient to conclude that there exists a correlation between the mean time of flare occurrence and spot modulation flux.

Chapter 6 --- Conclusions¶

Our data fell more in line with the null hypothesis than Martin et al's, but nonetheless demonstrated a noteworthy trend of flares occurring more when the spot modulation flux is positive. Because the bootstrapping test still produced a p-value of less than 0.01, the data still supports this trend. However, the mean of the data has become significantly lower since 2023 due to more new flares occurring with very low negative values than high positive ones. This is reflected by the differences between medians and means for the samples; the median decreased by 22.0% (7.55e-4 to 5.89e-4) between the 2023 data and the new data, while the mean decreased by fully 51.4% (from 2.69e-4 to 1.31e-4). As a result, the t test produced a p-value of 0.206, suggesting that although the mean is higher than that of the null hypothesis, it is not statistically significant.
